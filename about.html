About me

Storage is always there. Every system I've cared about — distributed systems, databases, edge computing — eventually turns into a storage problem.

How I got here

It started with database systems in undergrad. I liked that a DBMS is where the abstractions stop and something has to actually be written to disk, correctly, every time.

Then I got an opportunity at Veritas, and storage stopped being a subject and became the job. Enterprise backup systems, media servers, replication, high availability, disaster recovery — two years of production NetBackup for large enterprise customers. I worked directly with the people on the other end of a failed restore, which is a fast way to learn what reliability actually means to someone.

I came to UIC for exposure to AI, and for a more personal reason: I was curious how technology is built and used here, and how people live around it. That curiosity was most of the reason I moved countries.

Then Argonne National Laboratory — massive parallel file systems, real supercomputers, and a summer spent benchmarking vector databases at a scale I hadn't worked at before.

Why storage

Storage evolved quietly while everyone watched the models. Now AI is making it the interesting problem again: a model is only as good as what you can store, index, and retrieve fast enough to use. Vector databases sit exactly on that line, which is why most of my work has drifted there.

What Argonne changed

It taught me to think at scale, and showed me how AI is reshaping the way research actually gets done — interdisciplinary teams, shared infrastructure, people from completely different fields using the same machines.

It also grounded me. I spent a summer around excellent researchers and engineers, which recalibrates your sense of what good looks like.

And it taught me to be careful with my own conclusions. When IVF recall degraded on our multimodal data, my first explanation for why turned out to be wrong. The real cause was norm asymmetry between modalities in the fused embedding space. I corrected it in the final report rather than leave a convenient answer standing. That's the part of the internship I think about most.

Outside the work

I'm a social person — working with people is the part of engineering I enjoy most, not the part I tolerate. Outside of that, I'm figuring out how to live stress-free and healthy. I treat that as my own responsibility: I'm the CEO of my life.

What I'm aiming for is simple enough to say and hard enough to do: a good team with good people, learning, building, failing, and dancing with colleagues. Family. Knowing my own mind and body properly. Happiness that isn't contingent on the next offer.
